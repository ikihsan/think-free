=== TITLE ===
Why does pointer-to-member dereference operator have lower precedence than regular member 
access?

=== QUESTION ===
The expression a.b.*c.d is parsed as (a.b).*(c.d) due to the . operator having higher prec
edence than the .* operator. Why do these operators have different precedence? What led to
 this decision? What are the advantages? 

 a.b.*c.d 
 (a.b).*(c.d) 
 . 
 .*

=== KEY ANSWER ===
The . operator and the -> operator aren't normal infix operators because the right hand pa
rt of either one is not a general expression (you aren't allowed to put anything in parent
heses in there, unlike with regular infix operators). Rather, . <name> and -> <name> are p
ostfix operators. Various operator precedence guides group . and -> together with infix op
erators, but this is not entirely correct. 
 (Language lawyer hat on) <name> is actually template_opt id-expression or, in C++26, also
 a splice-expression . The standard doesn't use the term "postfix operator", it calls expr
essions with . and -> at the top level "postfix expressions". (Language lawyer hat off) St
ill if we call postfix ++ a postfix operator, it's reasonable to call -> <name> the same w
ay, because it is grouped together with postfix ++ and postfix -- and "indexing" (e.g., ar
rayName[5] ) and "function call". 
 Postfix operators can have precedence like infix operators, but one cannot assign precede
nce to . as if it were an infix operator. Rather, one must assign precedence to the whole 
. <name> thing. Otherwise, things like a.b+c could parse to a.(b+c) which wouldn't make an
y sense. 
 In C++, all postfix operators have strictly higher precedence than all infix operators. T
his is a reasonable decision shared by most if not all mainstream languages. 
While it is theoretically possible to have a.*b.c to parse as (a.*b).c , this parse would 
seem alien to C users which were the primary target audience of C++, and also would raise 
many further questions about a.*b++ and the like.
