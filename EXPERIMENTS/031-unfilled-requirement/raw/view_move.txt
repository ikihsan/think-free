As someone who is in the midst of migrating from Svelte 4 to Svelte 5, I definitely feel the pain of syntax changes. However, I'd argue that `on:change` -> `onchange` isn't frivolous. A real world example: in Svelte 4, I had a custom input component that wrapped an   element. To be able to do ` ` took a bunch of event forwarding boiler plate (or magic `on:change` syntax to forward that one event). When I wanted to also forward blur events I had to go through the same process. Now in Svelte 5 I can just use `const { foo, ...rest } = $props()` then spread with ` ` and I get all events forwarded for free. They could have kept the original `on:change` syntax, but that would have made property access more painful (who wants to call `rest['on:change']`?) and would have broken all my other components that I haven't migrated yet. The same goes for slots to snippets. Being able to pass snippets around like props has allowed me to simplify tons of components where slots were limiting. As a solo dev working part time on a Javascript project, I definitely hate some of the ecosystem churn, but this is one refactor I'm happy to do, and I'd say the same about most of the Svelte 5 changes.

COMMENT:
Back in the day when analog modems were becoming obsolete, there was a hue and cry from broadband enthusiasts that cable modem technology was inferior infrastructure. These folks all agreed that DOCSIS and cable modems would suffer crippling congestion in neighborhoods. That your cable modem's bandwidth would be shared bandwidth, shared on a medium with all your neighbors up to that head-end, and that this shared medium would inevitably be oversold many times over, and that cable-modem ISPs could never possibly meet bandwidth demands on these local networks. In contrast, DSL technology was sold as "never congested, never shared": that is, your copper pairs would wire up directly to the wire center, and have a dedicated connection into the ISP's backbone (and that ISP was experienced in telecommunications, rather than the entertainment industry) and therefore, DSL was always superior due to its lack of shared mediums. It was a weird argument, because inevitably, every provider's core network is shared to some degree, and cable modems really have not suffered congestion beyond the ordinary. Conversely, DSL also had its strategies for aggregation, overselling, and shared media. But a lot of people believed those stories about topology myths. Personally, I switched from ADSL to cable modem over 18 years ago, and I haven't regretted it. Very stable, always available and reliable, and plenty of bandwidth to spare, for my applications.
