# E115 M1 — the population, and what the extractor got wrong

Run 2026-10-12 against `PROTOCOL.md`. Raw data: `parts-title29.jsonl`
(328 parts, 7,145 sections), `m1-summary.json`. Standard library only.

| of which the extractor flagged `divergent` | **15 (0.185)** | K1 ≥ 0.10 → **PASS** |

**K1 passes.** There is a real population: 81 threshold spans in one title of
the CFR, and the counting rule is not plain headcount for a meaningful
fraction of them. There is no kill here — this is the direction in which the
line stays open.

## K2 did not fire, but the metric it named was wrong and I am recording that

The protocol declared `extraction_coverage < 0.50` as a kill gate and the
harvest reported **0.0084**. That number is the fraction of *all* sections
labelled, and **it is the wrong statistic**: most CFR sections contain no
applicability threshold at all, so a low value is the expected shape of a
correct extractor and not a defect.

Reporting 0.0084 as if it were a coverage failure would have been the exact
error F116 describes — a statistic that cannot fail, standing in for one that
can. The right instrument check here is **precision and recall on
hand-labelled rows**, not a fraction labelled. M1b measures that. This is a
correction of the protocol's own gate, recorded rather than quietly applied.

## M1b — hand-read of the 15 flagged "divergent" spans

Every span the extractor flagged as divergent, read against its own context:

| verdict | count | what it is |
|---|---|---|
| **real counting-rule divergence** | **11** | the numerator or the window is genuinely not plain headcount |
| **artifact** | **4** | see below |

The four artifacts are all the same two shapes, and both are the extractor's
fault, not the regulation's:

- **`"000 or more employees"` (§ 1614.702, § 4262.7, § 4262.8).** The real text
  is *"which has 1,000 or more employees"*. The extractor's `\b` boundary
  matched between the comma and the `0`. A word boundary is the wrong
  boundary when the number is comma-grouped. This is F116's substring class
  again, in the exact place the E113 protocol said to guard against it, and it
  survived because I anchored on `\b` instead of on the number token.
- **`"less than employees"` / `"greater than employees"` (§ 1614.203).** The
  regulation says *"salaries less than employees at the GS-11, step 1"* —
  a comparison between two *salaries*, not a headcount. The threshold
  vocabulary matched two nouns that happened to be adjacent.

**So the honest precision figure is 11 of 15 = 0.733 on the rows the
extractor thought were interesting** — which is the hard half, since these
were pre-selected for containing a numerator or window token. On the full set
of 81 spans the extractor is a *candidate finder*, and its candidates were
hand-read rather than trusted.

**This is the one number in this record that most resembles the E064/E089
near-miss package names** (93 of 576 plausible near-misses were real, different
artifacts): a cheap text matcher produces candidates and the candidate set
needs a human or a checker. It is not a candidate for anything on its own.

## What the counting rules actually say (read, not regexed)

The divergences are not theoretical. Three real cases, quoted:

**FMLA — § 825.104(a).** *"employs 50 or more employees for each working day
during each of 20 or more calendar workweeks in the current or preceding
calendar year."* The threshold is not a headcount: it is **50 on every working
day**, sustained across **20+ workweeks**, counted over **two calendar years**,
and § 825.111 adds a **75-mile** worksite test. A company with 55 employees in
November and 20 in March is **not** covered. Plain headcount says it is.

**FMLA — § 825.105(a).** Coverage counting borrows the FLSA § 3(g) definition
of "employ", which the section says reaches *"to suffer or permit to work"* —
**broader than common law**. Contractors and subsidiaries count in ways a
headcount does not capture.

**OSHA recordkeeping — § 1904.1(a)(1).** *"If your company had 10 or fewer
employees **at all times during the last calendar year**"*. The exemption
turns on **peak-ever employment over a year**, not current headcount — and
§ 1904.1(b)(1) adds that it is the **entire company**, not the establishment.
§ 1904.2 adds a second, independent exemption keyed to **NAICS industry code**,
which is not a number at all.

**Why this is the finding.** In all three the headcount and the answer point
in *opposite* directions, and in § 1904.1 the divergence is about the *past*.
An incumbent that answers "do you need OSHA records?" by counting today's
employees is wrong in the direction that costs money, and the correction lives
in a different section from the threshold.

## What this does not establish

- Nothing about whether anyone gets these wrong. That is M2, and the expected
  outcome is that they do not (E062, E092, E114 all closed that way).
- One title. Title 29 is the strongest case for this population because OSHA
  and FMLA are employee-count rules; a title with dollar thresholds may not
  have the same shape, and this says nothing about them.
- 81 spans across 60 sections is **not** a large population for a product on
  its own. It is large enough to test the question. If M2 finds the incumbent
  correct, the number does not matter and the line closes regardless.
- No recall figure. 25 of the 60 threshold-bearing sections were read; the rest
  are in the JSONL with their spans and contexts for anyone to check.
